The Fallacy of the "Rough" MVP

April 18, 2026 · 5 min read
C
Carla Rinaldi · April 18, 2026 · 5 min read

In the fast-paced world of tech startups and product management, there is a persistent tension that every founder and product leader eventually faces: the trade-off between speed and craftsmanship. We are constantly told to "fail fast," to embrace the Minimum Viable Product (MVP), and to iterate based on market feedback. This philosophy, popularized by the Lean Startup movement, has become the de facto operating system for Silicon Valley and beyond. However, as I reflect on the evolution of product development and the increasing sophistication of global markets, I find myself questioning whether our obsession with speed has come at the expense of true engineering excellence.

For many years, the "lean" approach was synonymous with "rough." The goal was to get something—anything—into the hands of users to see if they would bite. But the modern consumer is no longer satisfied with rough edges. Whether it is a SaaS platform, a mobile app, or a physical consumer good, the bar for "viability" has shifted toward "lovability." This shift requires a return to craftsmanship, an attention to detail that mirrors the precision of high-end engineering, even when we are in the experimental phase of a startup.

The Fallacy of the "Rough" MVP

The original intent of the MVP was never to release a broken or subpar product. Rather, it was about identifying the smallest set of features that could provide value and generate learning. Somewhere along the way, this was misinterpreted as a license to cut corners. In reality, the most successful innovation teams—the ones coached by legends like Scott Cook or those operating within high-growth ecosystems—understand that quality is a feature in itself. If the user experience is friction-filled, you aren't testing the "value hypothesis"; you are merely testing the user's patience.

When we look at platform experiments, the stakes are even higher. A platform is a promise of reliability and scalability. If you launch a platform experiment that lacks the necessary precision, you risk alienating the very developers and early adopters you need to build your ecosystem. This is where the concept of "High-Fidelity Prototyping" becomes critical. We need to move beyond wireframes and toward experiences that feel real, even if the backend is still being held together by manual processes (the "Wizard of Oz" approach). This high-fidelity requirement isn't just about aesthetics; it's about building trust.

"Innovation is not just about the new idea; it’s about the obsessive execution of that idea until it feels inevitable to the user."

This obsession with execution can be seen in various industries outside of software. Consider the world of precision manufacturing or even the high-end secondary markets. In these spaces, the difference between a "good" product and a "great" one is measured in microns. For instance, the level of detail found in an AP super clone demonstrates how even the most complex mechanical designs can be reverse-engineered and replicated with startling accuracy when there is a deep understanding of the original's craftsmanship. For a product manager, the lesson here isn't about replication, but about the standard of excellence. If a third-party manufacturer can achieve that level of fidelity in a physical product, we have no excuse for delivering sloppy software experiences under the guise of "being lean."

Engineering Excellence and the "Platform" Mindset

Building a product is one thing; building a platform is another. In my previous discussions on platform experiments and "Chicken and Egg" problems, I’ve emphasized that the initial value proposition must be lopsidedly in favor of the early adopter. To achieve this, the product must feel like a finished work of art, even if the feature set is narrow. This requires a "Platform Mindset"—a commitment to building robust foundations from day one.

Product managers often struggle with this because it feels like it slows them down. They see the "Lean Startup" as a race to the finish line, but they forget that the race is actually a marathon of many sprints. If you don't stop to maintain your "engine"—your codebase, your design system, your user research methodology—you will eventually break down. This is where the coaching of innovation teams becomes so vital. A great coach doesn't just push the team to move faster; they push the team to move *better*.

  • Focus on the Core: Identify the one thing your product must do better than anyone else and make it perfect.
  • Automate the Feedback Loop: Use AI and analytics to understand where users are struggling before they even tell you.
  • Respect the Craft: Whether it's clean code or a seamless UI, never apologize for taking the time to do it right.

By balancing the "lean" approach with a commitment to engineering excellence, we create products that don't just survive the experiment phase but thrive in the competitive marketplace. We move from building "minimum viable" to "minimum incredible."

The Role of AI in Scaling Quality

As we look toward the future, Artificial Intelligence is playing an increasingly central role in how we manage this balance between speed and quality. AI isn't just a buzzword to throw into a pitch deck; it is a fundamental shift in how we build and iterate. In the past, high-quality QA (Quality Assurance) or deep user research took weeks. Today, AI-driven tools can simulate user behavior, identify edge cases in code, and synthesize thousands of customer feedback points in minutes.

This allows us to maintain a "lean" velocity without sacrificing the craftsmanship that users expect. AI can act as the "coach" for the developer, suggesting optimizations and catching errors before they reach production. It can act as the "designer's assistant," ensuring that every component of the UI adheres to the established design system. In this sense, AI is the ultimate tool for the modern craftsman. It handles the repetitive, high-volume tasks, freeing the human creator to focus on the nuance, the strategy, and the "soul" of the product.

However, we must be careful. AI can also lead to a "sea of sameness" if we rely on it too heavily for creativity. The most successful startups will be those that use AI to handle the precision and the scale, while keeping the human touch at the center of the product's vision. Much like the trek up to Triund or the base camp of Mt. Dhauladhar, the tools (the gear, the maps, the GPS) are essential, but the journey itself is a human experience. It requires grit, intuition, and a personal connection to the landscape.

In conclusion, entrepreneurship is not a choice between being fast or being good. It is the art of being both. By embracing the principles of the Lean Startup while maintaining a relentless focus on craftsmanship and precision, we can build products that aren't just "functional," but are truly remarkable. Whether we are building the next great AI platform or a niche consumer product, the goal remains the same: to create something that stands the test of time and provides genuine value to the world.

As we continue to experiment, build, and scale, let's not forget the lessons of the masters—whether they are innovation coaches like Scott Cook or the anonymous artisans of precision manufacturing. The details aren't just details; they are the product.
